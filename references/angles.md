# Angle Reference

Full detail for the 7+3 taxonomy. The core skill (`SKILL.md`) is enough for routine work; read this file when two angles look similar, the niche is unfamiliar, or you need more examples.

These are practitioner content patterns, not formal journalism categories. They are useful because they change the reader's entry point, not because they map to a academic taxonomy. `Conditions` in particular is a decision structure, not a perspective.

---

## 1. Myth vs Reality

**Pattern:** Everyone says X. In reality, Y works - and here is why.

**Target:** Sacred cows of the niche - established dogmas nobody dares to question. What beliefs are outdated but unchallenged? What "truths" make everyone in the niche silently suffer?

**Why it works:** You touch the untouchable. It triggers emotion, debate, and sharing. Some readers defend the sacred cow; others feel relieved that someone finally said it.

**Examples:**

- "Everyone says the key to good therapy is unconditional ethics. Here is when that backfires."
- "The common advice is to post every day. Here is why consistency is killing your content quality."
- "Everyone says handmade mugs are overpriced. Here is what [X] hours of craft actually look like."

**Watch out:** The myth must be real and prevalent. If nobody believes it, the hook becomes a strawman.

---

## 2. Paradox

**Pattern:** The more X, the worse Y. / A counter-intuitive but real relationship.

**Target:** Assumptions people hold that are logically reversed from reality.

**Why it works:** The brain notices pattern violations. A paradox forces cognitive re-evaluation, which makes it sticky.

**Examples:**

- "The harder you try on each video, the worse your content performs overall."
- "The more features you add, the fewer customers convert."
- "The cheaper your service, the harder it is to sell."

**Watch out:** The contradiction must be genuine. A forced rhetorical inversion reads as clickbait.

---

## 3. Level Error

**Pattern:** You think the problem is X. The real cause is Y - on a completely different level.

**Target:** Symptoms people are treating while the root cause sits untouched in another domain.

**Why it works:** It reframes the reader's entire understanding of the problem and delivers insight rather than surface advice.

**Examples:**

- "You think you are not creating content because of imposter syndrome. The real reason: you lack raw material and do not know what to say."
- "You think clients are not booking because your prices are too high. The real cause: you never built trust before showing the price."
- "You think you have an audience problem. You actually have a positioning problem."

**Watch out:** Do not use this to dismiss a real condition ("you think you have imposter syndrome, but your skills just need work"). Reframing must be compassionate and evidence-based.

**Naming note:** The label borrows from systems thinking (logical levels), but the pattern here is specifically symptom vs root cause. Do not claim academic precision.

---

## 4. Price of Solution

**Pattern:** Yes, X works. But here is what you are really paying - and nobody talks about it.

**Target:** Hidden costs, trade-offs, and uncomfortable truths behind a solution, choice, or delay.

**Why it works:** Honest trade-off talk builds credibility. People are tired of "X solves everything" pitches and want the full picture.

**Examples:**

- "Yes, content marketing works. The price: it is a full-time production operation - scripting, analytics, filming, editing."
- "Yes, outsourcing saves time. The price: you lose direct customer signal and the product drifts away from users."
- "Yes, a cheaper accountant saves [$X] a year. The bill arrives at tax season."

**Watch out:** Do not invent the cost. If the number is unknown, use a placeholder or describe the cost qualitatively.

---

## 5. Conditions

**Pattern:** If situation A, do X. If situation B, do Y. (Decision tree / segmentation.)

**Target:** Audiences who need "does this apply to me?" answered immediately.

**Why it works:** People scan for themselves. When they find their situation, they lock in. It also screens out bad-fit leads.

**Examples:**

- "If you have a team, delegate content. If you are solo, batch-produce one format you can sustain."
- "If your mole looks like this, see a dermatologist. If it looks like this, it is normal." (Health example: educational only; never a diagnosis.)
- "If you sell services, lead with case studies. If you sell products, lead with transformations."

**Watch out:** Branches must be concrete and actionable. Generic branches ("if you want results, work hard") fail.

---

## 6. Hidden Driver

**Pattern:** The real reason X happens is Y - impossible to guess without deep experience in this niche.

**Target:** Non-obvious causal chains visible only to an expert with accumulated pattern recognition.

**Why it works:** It delivers genuine insider insight and makes the reader feel they unlocked secret knowledge. High "aha" factor.

**Examples:**

- "Clients are not booking your junior stylists - not because they are worse, but because your entire page only talks about YOU."
- "Your ad creative is not the problem. The problem is you are showing people a product for a job they do not realize they need done."
- "Finance teams are not lazy. Their bonus depends on zero errors, and they do not trust your tool yet."

**Watch out:** The driver must be plausible and, ideally, verifiable. Do not invent a hidden mechanism to sound deep.

---

## 7. Contrary Thesis

**Pattern:** Do not do X. Do the opposite. Here is the honest case for why.

**Target:** Mainstream advice that has calcified into unexamined dogma.

**Why it works:** Going against the current generates heat and debate, but only an honest contrarian take earns lasting trust.

**Examples:**

- "Do not niche down. Generalize first, find what resonates, then narrow."
- "Do not optimize for SEO. Optimize for one real human reading, and search engines will follow."
- "Do not build an audience. Build relationships with 10 people who would pay you today."

**Watch out:** Never manufacture a contrarian position for engagement. If the mainstream advice is right for most readers, the honest move is a Conditions angle instead.

---

## 8. Historical Lens

**Pattern:** How was it in the past? What did people do before the current default?

**Target:** Audiences who enjoy origin stories and pop-science history.

**Why it works:** History gives context, authority, and a narrative arc. It also makes a familiar topic feel new.

**Examples:**

- "What fragrances did Marie Antoinette wear at Versailles?"
- "This centuries-old Japanese firing technique still cannot be replicated by machines."
- "Before dashboards, managers tracked performance with [X]. Here is what changed."

**Watch out:** Localize the history. Versailles does not resonate with every audience; use the market's own heritage.

---

## 9. Future Lens

**Pattern:** Where is this going in 5-10 years? What developments are already underway?

**Target:** Readers deciding whether to act now or wait.

**Why it works:** It creates urgency and positions the expert as a guide to what comes next.

**Examples:**

- "What will this niche look like in 5-10 years?"
- "The job that will disappear first is not the one everyone fears."
- "Three changes already visible in [market] that will reset pricing by [year]."

**Watch out:** Do not present predictions as facts. Label scenarios and assumptions; use `[year]` and `[source]` placeholders when the data is missing.

---

## 10. News Hook / Story Bridge

**Pattern:** Take a real news item, fact, or story from the niche. Tell it. Bridge it to the lesson.

**Target:** Audiences who need immediate relevance and a reason to read now.

**Why it works:** A concrete story lowers the entry cost and borrows the news item's attention.

**Examples:**

- The story of how a Japanese company invented a medical massager that consumers repurposed for an entirely different use, bridged to the lesson that businesses often misunderstand their real customer use-cases.
- "A court ruling this week changed [X]. Here is what it means for your [role]."
- "One line in a company's annual report explains why your [category] is about to get cheaper."

**Watch out:** Verify the news item and date. Never invent a study, ruling, or launch. If you cannot verify it, use a different angle.

## Distinguishing close angles

### Myth vs Reality vs Contrary Thesis vs Hidden Driver

- **Myth vs Reality** corrects a false belief: "Everyone believes X, but the truth is Y." The reader *thinks* X; you reveal Y.
- **Contrary Thesis** prescribes opposite behavior: "The standard advice is X, but you should do NOT-X." The reader *does* X; you tell them to stop and replace it with Y.
- **Hidden Driver** reveals a hidden cause: "X happens, but not for the reason you think." The reader sees the effect; you expose the mechanism.
- **Level Error** says the problem is misdiagnosed at the wrong level: "You are treating a symptom; the cause is elsewhere."

Quick test:

1. Is the reader holding a false belief? → Myth vs Reality.
2. Is the reader doing something you want them to stop? → Contrary Thesis.
3. Is the reader seeing an effect but missing the cause? → Hidden Driver or Level Error.
4. Is the cause in a different domain (not just a different variable)? → Level Error.

### Paradox vs Level Error

- **Paradox:** two variables move in opposite directions (more A → less B). A structural contradiction.
- **Level Error:** the problem is misdiagnosed - symptom vs root cause. A category mistake.

If you can draw a graph with A on one axis and B on the other, it is a Paradox. If you can draw a chain from symptom to root cause, it is a Level Error.

### Conditions vs Price of Solution

- **Conditions:** the answer depends on the reader's situation. The value is segmentation.
- **Price of Solution:** the answer is the same for everyone, but the hidden cost differs from the advertised benefit. The value is honesty about trade-offs.

Do not use Conditions when there are no real branches. Do not use Price of Solution when the real issue is fit.

## Hook-writing notes

- One sentence. Put the tension in the first 8-12 words.
- Concrete nouns beat abstractions: "tax season" beats "financial complexity".
- Use the reader's language, not the industry's internal vocabulary, unless the jargon itself signals insider status.
- A number is only persuasive if it is real. Otherwise use `[X]` and flag it.
- If the hook needs a two-minute setup, it belongs in long-form, not short-form.

---

## Cultural adaptation notes

- **Contrary Thesis and Myth vs Reality** work best where debate and individualism are welcome. In markets where group harmony dominates, soften the frame to "an alternative view" rather than "everyone is wrong".
- **Paradox and Level Error** travel well because they rely on cognitive pattern violation, not cultural references.
- **Historical Lens** must use local history. A Japanese audience connects to Edo-period references; a Brazilian audience to local heritage; do not default to European examples.
- **Price of Solution** depends on whether the audience prioritizes risk mitigation or aspiration. When uncertain, present both the cost of doing X and the cost of not doing X.
- **Do not infer culture from nationality alone.** Use the specific audience, market, and evidence. When uncertain, prefer universal mechanisms or decision framing.
